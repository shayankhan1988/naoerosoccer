Our story

How football arrived late on Pleasant Island, why it is here to stay, and the island it belongs to.

About the NSF

Starting from zero, with everything to play for

Naoero is one of the few UN member states that is not a member of FIFA, and the only one never to have played a representative match. Australian Rules Football is the national sport. Football is widely watched, but there are few opportunities to play it, in schools or in organised competition, partly because the island has no football pitch.

The Naoero Soccer Federation is rooted in the country's boldness (anga eiy), bravery (timu) and resilience (anga dogin oeo). Setting up a sustainable and lasting football structure is not an everyday task: we literally start from zero, in a country that has a lot to offer, but also a lot to suffer.

There is momentum. Football is on the rise in neighbouring Pacific nations such as Kiribati, Tuvalu, Niue and the Marshall Islands, and it is emerging in countries where climate change threatens their very existence. That calls for action on climate resilience and for something that sets the stage for international attention: football.

We always work in accordance with local customs and networks on Naoero, its Oceanian diaspora and our worldwide football-loving community. Our main goal is a lasting legacy for the girls and boys of Naoero.

Timeline

  1. 1798

    Pleasant Island

    British captain John Fearn sights Naoero and names it Pleasant Island. Twelve tribes have lived here for thousands of years.

  2. 1888

    Colonial rule begins

    Germany annexes the island; after 1914 it passes to Australian, British and New Zealand administration. Football never takes hold. Australian Rules becomes the dominant sport.

  3. 1906

    Phosphate

    Mining begins on Topside, the island's interior plateau. It will shape Naoero's economy, landscape and population for a century.

  4. 1960s

    The miners' league

    Workers from other Pacific islands, employed in the phosphate mines, set up a six-team football league. It fades away together with the mining.

  5. 1968

    Independence

    On 31 January Naoero becomes the world's smallest independent republic. Organised football stays almost undocumented, apart from two representative matches.

  6. 2018

    Interest returns

    The Nauru Amateur Soccer Association and the Nauru Soccer Association are formed. Neither manages to organise matches.

  7. 2023

    The NSF is founded

    The Naoero Soccer Federation is established under the Naoero Olympic Committee, with members inside and outside the country.

  8. 2024

    Crest and kit

    A new crest and the first national kit in Naoero's history, made with kit partner Stingz.

  9. 2026

    One Child One Ball

    The federation launches its first campaign: an eco-friendly football for every child on Naoero aged 4 to 14.

  10. 2026

    Meaningful relationships

    The NSF global members visit Naoero to pave the way for our first Grassroots Programme.

Resilience

The challenges we work on

Naoero is on the front line of climate change and faces some of the toughest health statistics in the world. The island's own Higher Ground Initiative sets out what a resilient Naoero needs. Our football work serves the same goals.

Good health and well-being

Sports participation, a health curriculum in schools and simply playing the game reduce the risks of obesity and related disease.

Education and training

Our grassroots curriculum runs through schools and community centres, and trains local coaches, referees and board members.

Water security

Our future football ground will hold water tanks for fresh water, next to its dressing rooms and office.

Food security

The ground will be extended with a greenhouse to produce food, in step with the island's own climate programmes.

Natural restoration

A climate-resilient turf and surroundings make the pitch a haven for biodiversity rather than a drain on it.

Community shelter

In a nation prone to extreme rainfall and floods, the canteen beside the pitch doubles as a shelter for the community.

The star

Twelve points, twelve tribes

The white star on Naoero's flag has twelve points, one for each of the island's original tribes. Two of them, Irutsi and Iwi, no longer survive, but their points stay on the star. Nobody is left out of the team.

    Fourteen districts, one ring road

      What our colours mean

      A flag you can read like a map

      The blue

      The Pacific Ocean on every side. On Naoero the sea is road, pantry and horizon, and it is the ground colour of our shirt.

      The gold line

      The equator. Naoero lies just south of it, which is why the star sits below the line. On this site the line doubles as the halfway line of a pitch.

      The white star

      The island itself, placed where it sits on the globe: a little south of the line and west of the date line.

      Traditions we carry

      Older than any scoreboard

      The frigatebird

      The frigatebird flies on our crest and stands on Naoero's coat of arms. For centuries Naoeroans have caught and tamed these great seabirds, a contest of skill, patience and pride between families and districts. It is the island's oldest sport.

      String figures

      Naoero is renowned across the Pacific for its string figures: intricate patterns stretched between the hands, each telling a story. The line patterns between the sections of this site are drawn from that tradition.

      Angam Day, 26 October

      Angam means homecoming and celebration. The day marks the moments the Naoeroan people reached 1,500, the number judged necessary to survive, first in 1932 and again in 1949 after the losses of the war years. It is a national day of resilience.

      Topside and the pinnacles

      A century of phosphate mining left the interior a forest of limestone pinnacles. It is part of who we are: an island that gave a great deal and is now building something of its own.
